Modular Arithmetic

CORE

The problem they give you

They will not say “Modular Arithmetic.” They describe this:

Given: Huge counts that must come back modulo m (often 10^9+7).

Find: Add, subtract, multiply, and divide in that ring — divide means multiply by an inverse.

Tiny example: (5 − 8) mod 7 = 4, not −3. 6/2 mod 7 = 3. You cannot integer-divide after reducing.

Name of the tool

Work in Z/mZ: add and multiply then reduce. Division is multiply by the modular inverse, which exists iff gcd(a,m)=1. Watch negative residues and overflow.

Why this tool

Almost every 'return the answer modulo 10^9+7' interview is modular arithmetic plus a DP or combinatorics.

Approach

  1. Normalize: ((x % m) + m) % m.
  2. add(a,b)= (a+b)%m with a wide type; mul as 128-bit or careful JS number (use BigInt if n is large).
  3. div(a,b)= mul(a, modInverse(b,m)).

Whiteboard prompts

These are problem statements. The algorithm name is your answer, not the title of the question.

  • Return the answer modulo 10^9+7.
  • How do you divide by 2 when the answer is a residue?
  • A subtraction goes negative before the mod — what do you return?

You say this first

Work in Z/mZ: add and multiply then reduce. Division is multiply by the modular inverse, which exists iff gcd(a,m)=1. Watch negative residues and overflow. I'd dry-run a tiny case first, then say the invariant and the complexity (O(1) add/mul; O(log m) inverse time, O(1) space).

Offer the next notch: add(a,b)= (a+b)%m with a wide type; mul as 128-bit or careful JS number (use BigInt if n is large).. Tradeoff: The problem wants the exact integer, not a residue. If the recurrence has a division by 2, ask whether 2 has an inverse (MOD odd ⇒ yes).

More notes if you want them

Almost every 'return the answer modulo 10^9+7' interview is modular arithmetic plus a DP or combinatorics. If you divide by 2 in the integers and then mod, you are wrong — you must multiply by the inverse of 2. If you subtract and get a negative, add MOD before reducing. These two bugs fail hidden tests constantly.

Use when: Huge counts, combinatorics, geometric series of ways, hash polynomials.

Skip when: The problem wants the exact integer, not a residue.

Trap: Negative modulo.
