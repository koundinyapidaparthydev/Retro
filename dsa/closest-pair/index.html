Closest Pair of Points

ADV

The problem they give you

They will not say “Closest Pair of Points.” They describe this:

Given: Points on a plane. Checking every pair is too slow.

Find: The two closest points and their distance. After both halves, only a thin strip around the middle line can still beat the record.

Tiny example: Points (0,0), (3,4), (1,1). Closest is (0,0)-(1,1), distance √2, not (0,0)-(3,4).

Name of the tool

Divide the plane by x-median, recurse, then scan a strip of width δ around the midline. The strip has a 7-neighbor property that keeps the combine linearithmic.

Why this tool

Closest pair is the divide-and-conquer problem that is not a sort.

Approach

  1. If n ≤ 3, brute-force distances and return the min pair.
  2. Split the x-sorted array at mid.
  3. Collect points with |x - midX| < δ; they are already y-sortable in linear time by merging y-order.

Whiteboard prompts

These are problem statements. The algorithm name is your answer, not the title of the question.

  • Return both the pair and the distance.
  • 1-D version: sort and check neighbors only.
  • Two points sit on top of each other. Distance 0.

You say this first

Divide the plane by x-median, recurse, then scan a strip of width δ around the midline. The strip has a 7-neighbor property that keeps the combine linearithmic. I'd dry-run a tiny case first, then say the invariant and the complexity (O(n log n) time, O(n) for strip buffers and recursion space).

Offer the next notch: Split the x-sorted array at mid. Recurse left and right; δ = min(δL, δR).. Tradeoff: High dimension — the strip packing constant explodes and you use trees or hashing. Draw the midline and the strip. Say 'constant neighbors in the strip' before you code.

More notes if you want them

Closest pair is the divide-and-conquer problem that is not a sort. It shows up in computational-geometry interviews and in 'beat the obvious O(n²) all-pairs' discussions. The insight you must teach: after both halves have minimum δ, any closer pair straddling the line lives in a thin strip, and inside a sorted-by-y strip each point only needs a constant number of neighbors. Miss that and your combine is quadratic.

Use when: Closest pair in 2D (or low dimension) in O(n log n).

Skip when: High dimension — the strip packing constant explodes and you use trees or hashing.

Trap: Comparing every pair in the strip (O(n²) combine).
